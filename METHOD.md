# Method

Written before the findings, deliberately. Limits drafted after the analysis have a
way of shrinking to fit whatever turned up.

## The question

`the-human-element` studied 10,042 public security incidents and found that a large
share of them run through interfaces, defaults and warnings that set people up to
fail. It could not say *why* anyone proceeded, because incident records store
outcomes, not reasoning.

This study asks the other half: **what do technical people do when a security
warning has no answer they can give, and how do they account for it?**

### The question changed, and that is disclosed rather than hidden

The question I started with was narrower: *when technical people decide to click
through a security warning, how do they account for that decision afterwards?*

The corpus could not support it. After retrieval, items containing an account of
someone deciding to proceed numbered about ten. Items about warnings that could not
be satisfied in the first place numbered in the hundreds.

Continuing with the original framing would have meant building an argument on ten
items. Instead the question was reframed to what the data could answer — **before
coding began**, so the codes were not fitted to a conclusion — and the original
framing survives in the findings as its own result: the deliberate risk calculus
the security literature tends to assume is not what these practitioners talk about.

Note what neither question is. This is not "how often do people ignore warnings"
and not "which warnings get ignored most". This design cannot answer either, and
anywhere below where it looks like it might, it doesn't.

## Design

Netnographic. The corpus is public Hacker News discussion, retrieved with
[`fieldnotes`](https://github.com/uxrhimanshu/fieldnotes) and de-identified with
[`research-deid`](https://github.com/uxrhimanshu/research-deid). Analysis is open
coding to a codebook, then a thematic read across the codes.

`SAMPLING.md` is the retrieval record. It is generated by the tool, committed
unedited, and it is the document to read before believing anything in `FINDINGS.md`.

### Why Hacker News

The population is the point rather than a convenience. People who dismiss
certificate warnings are disproportionately people competent enough to understand
what the warning says — the interesting cases are not naive users being fooled, but
expert users making a considered decision to proceed. HN is dense with exactly that
population talking about exactly that moment.

The cost is that it generalises to nobody else, which is stated again below because
it is the single most likely way this study gets misread.

### Retrieval, and the screen that did not work

Threads were retrieved by phrase search across twelve query terms. `SAMPLING.md`
records the funnel.

Search matches a whole thread, so a product announcement that used the phrase
"security warning" once in a long blurb arrived with ninety replies about pricing
attached. In the first harvest that noise was most of the corpus.

The obvious fix was a lexical screen: keep only items that mention a query term.
It was built into `fieldnotes` as `--items-must-match`, and it did reduce a 2,350
item corpus to 103. **It also did not work**, and the failed attempt is kept in
`corpus-lexical-screen/` so that claim can be checked rather than taken on trust.

What it kept: link posts titled "Preventing Alert Fatigue" with no body text at
all; protocol arguments about certificate pinning between Kubernetes pods; a
discussion of how a school blocks DNS-over-HTTPS. All of them contain the words.
None of them contains a person accounting for a decision.

What it missed is worse and unmeasurable: every account written in wording the
phrase list did not anticipate — which, for a decision people describe in a
hundred different ways, is most of them.

The lesson is not subtle but it is worth stating, because the tempting move is to
keep tuning the phrase list. **The phenomenon is semantic and the retrieval is
lexical.** No amount of query tuning closes that gap. Something has to read the
items.

### The screen that was actually used

Every retrieved item was read and judged: does it contain an account of a person
deciding about a security warning, or does it merely contain the vocabulary?

`screening.csv` holds one row per retrieved item — the id, the decision, and one
of five declared reasons — and is committed with the corpus. `screen.py report`
verifies that every retrieved item has a decision and that no reason was invented
outside the declared set. So the screen is auditable item by item, which is more
than a keyword rule offers and considerably more than "items were selected for
relevance."

The reading was done by the same LLM-assisted process disclosed below for the
coding, with the same caveat: it is one analyst's judgement, published so it can
be disagreed with.

### The recall gate, and its known false negative

Reading 1,785 items individually was not proportionate, so a published recall gate
ran first: an item is read only if it contains one of a list of terms (in
`screen.py`, and applied in `gate.csv` for every retrieved item). It is deliberately
over-inclusive — 516 items passed — so that the *reading*, not the regex, makes the
relevance decision.

The gate has at least one false negative. One item judged relevant, `31409704`, had
failed it on a length threshold and was found only because an earlier pass had been
gated differently. It is marked `kept-though-below-gate` in `screening.csv` and
counted by `screen.py report`.

**The true false-negative rate is unknown**, because the 1,268 items below the gate
were not read. That is the honest cost of the gate and it is stated rather than
estimated.

A second limitation of the reading itself: for the keeps and for the certificate
and phishing threads, the decision was made item by item. For the mass of product
announcements the gate let through on words like "click" or "ignore", the judgement
was effectively made at thread level — one read of the thread disposed of its
replies. `screening.csv` records a decision per item either way, which slightly
overstates how granular the judgement was.

### De-identification, and what the tool got wrong here

Handles are pseudonymised by `fieldnotes` at collection. Comment bodies were then
run through `research-deid` — and the result is worth recording, because the tool
is mine and this was the first time it met data it was not designed for.

Run at full strength it changed 647 items with 3,205 replacements, and it was
badly wrong. It is tuned for interview transcripts, where a capitalised token is
usually a person. On a technical forum a capitalised token is usually a product, so
it pseudonymised **"Let's Encrypt", "Monte Carlo", "Google Analytics"** and dozens
of other product names as people. Applying that would have made the central finding
of this study unreadable, since it turns on what browsers do to self-signed
certificates versus Let's Encrypt ones.

Restricting it to phone numbers was no better: it matched software version numbers,
IP addresses, dates, and **Hacker News item ids** — the identifiers every quote in
`FINDINGS.md` is traced by.

So only email addresses were scrubbed: 22 replacements across 21 items, listed in
`deid-report.json`. Emails are unambiguous and are the one category here that is a
contactable address for a real person.

This is not a defect in `research-deid` so much as a boundary of it, and it has
been added to that tool's documented limits. It is also an argument for the review
pass that tool insists on: run without one, it would have silently rewritten the
corpus this study depends on.

Quotes in `FINDINGS.md` are reproduced verbatim after that scrub. Verbatim text is
still searchable: a distinctive sentence leads back to its author regardless of the
pseudonym on it. That is a residual risk of publishing quotes at all, not something
the pseudonymisation solves, and it is accepted here on the basis that every item is
a public post made under a public handle on a public forum.

## How the coding was done, and by whom

**First-pass codes were proposed by an LLM (Claude) over the full corpus. Every code
was then reviewed, renamed, merged or rejected by the author.** `codebook.md` records
what changed at that review.

This is disclosed prominently for two reasons. The first is ordinary research
integrity. The second is specific to this study: it would be absurd to write about
people taking a defensible-looking shortcut and then quietly take one.

What that means for the reader: the codebook is not the product of two independent
human coders, so **no inter-rater reliability statistic is reported**, because none
would mean anything. Treat the codes as one analyst's structure over the data,
assisted and then corrected, with the corpus published so the structure can be
argued with.

## What this design cannot support

- **No frequencies.** Nothing here supports "X% of engineers ignore warnings", or
  any claim that one reason is more common than another. A corpus assembled by
  phrase search from a self-selected forum has no denominator.
- **Posters are not users.** The overwhelming majority of people who read these
  threads never post. Nothing represents them.
- **Self-selection, twice over.** People write about a warning when the episode was
  unusual, unresolved or annoying — and they write about it on a forum that rewards
  a good story. Routine, uneventful compliance generates no text and is invisible
  here by construction.
- **Retrospective accounts are accounts.** Someone explaining why they clicked
  through is reconstructing a decision, often long after it, often in an argument.
  That is legitimate data about how the decision is *justified*. It is not evidence
  about what actually went through their head at the time.
- **Identity is unverified.** Claimed roles, employers and seniority are claims.
- **A recency sample wearing a date range.** Search returns newest-first, so a date
  floor plus a thread limit yields the most recent matching threads rather than a
  spread. `SAMPLING.md` reports this per query, with the gaps named.
- **Deleted and moderated content is invisible**, and a thread's most contested
  contributions are the likeliest to be missing.
- **One community, one language, one culture of work.** English-speaking, technical,
  US-weighted, and unusually confident about its own competence. Whatever this study
  finds is a finding about that community.

## Ethics

Every item is a public post under a public handle. Nothing was collected that
required an account to see, nothing private was accessed, and no author was
contacted.

Public is not the same as consenting to be studied. The mitigations here are
pseudonymisation at collection, a de-identification pass over the text, and no
attempt to link handles across platforms or to build any profile of an individual
poster. The re-identification risk that remains is the one carried by verbatim
quotation itself.

The author-to-pseudonym key is not published and is not in this repository.
